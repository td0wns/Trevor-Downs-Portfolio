# Trevor Downs portfolio voice guide

This guide records the habits that made the original portfolio copy feel machine-written and the rules used for the rewrite. No word or punctuation mark proves that AI wrote a page. The issue was the cumulative effect of repeated vocabulary, perfectly balanced sentences and the same case-study rhythm on every page.

## Phrases and words to watch

These terms are sometimes accurate, especially in technical descriptions, but they should not become default filler:

- “connected” or “joined-up” when the actual relationship can be named
- “governed” without stating the rule, permission or control involved
- “practical,” “strategic,” “meaningful,” “robust,” “scalable” and “actionable” without evidence
- “enabled,” “supported” and “delivered” when a more precise verb is available
- “at scale,” “in practice” and “end to end” as broad proof of impact
- “rather than” contrasts that explain what the work was not
- “turning X into Y,” “from X to Y” and “where X meets Y” headline formulas
- repeated strings such as “designed, built, tested and deployed”
- “What the project enabled/delivered/demonstrated” on every outcomes section

Technical terms such as governance, Salesforce, MCAE, segmentation and automation remain appropriate where they name the actual work.

## Punctuation and sentence patterns to watch

- Em dashes used repeatedly to introduce a qualification or polished aside
- Semicolons that join two equally balanced claims when a full stop would sound more natural
- Colon-led lists inside paragraphs when bullets or a direct sentence would be clearer
- Three short headline fragments with identical rhythm
- Long sentences made from several abstract noun phrases
- Repeated groups of three adjectives, capabilities or outcomes
- Every paragraph beginning with a past-tense project verb

## Voice for this portfolio

- Write in the first person when describing Trevor's decisions and work.
- Put the specific problem, choice or result before the professional framing.
- Prefer plain verbs: “I mapped,” “I checked,” “I trained,” “I changed.”
- Mix short and long sentences. Do not make every paragraph equally polished.
- Keep product names, figures, dates and scope exact.
- Explain technical work for an informed non-specialist without sanding off the detail.
- State caveats once, plainly, where they matter. Do not make defensive qualifications the dominant voice.
- Let personality come through in observation, judgement and occasional dry humour, not forced informality.
- Use headings that say what happened on that project instead of repeating one universal template.

## Quick editing test

Before publishing new copy, ask:

1. Could this sentence appear unchanged on someone else's portfolio?
2. Does the sentence name what Trevor actually did?
3. Is there a concrete noun, number or decision where an abstract phrase could be removed?
4. Have the surrounding sentences fallen into the same rhythm?
5. Is a caveat necessary here, or is the precise claim already clear?

If the answer to the first question is yes, rewrite. If the answer to either of the next two is no, add specificity.
